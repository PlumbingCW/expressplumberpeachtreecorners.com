Repiping in Peachtree Corners, GA

Repeated pinhole leaks, brown water and weak pressure all point to supply lines near the end of their life. Repiping replaces the failing pipes for good.

Signs Your Supply Pipes Are Near the End

One leak is a repair. A pattern is a decision.

Several clues suggest the lines are wearing out together. Brown or rusty water after a period of no use often points to corroding iron or galvanized pipe. Pressure that drops at every fixture can mean mineral buildup inside the pipe. Repeated pinhole leaks in copper show that the metal is failing in more than one place.

One leak can be repaired and forgotten. Three leaks in two years is a signal that the next one is coming. When that happens, a plumber should look at the whole system and not just the latest wet spot.

If you are not sure, our leak detection service pinpoints which leaks are isolated and which are part of a larger pattern.

Pipe Materials, Compared Honestly

Each material has strengths and limits, and none is perfect.

Common supply pipe materials
MaterialStrengthsWatch for
Copper.Long history, tolerates heat, widely repairable.Pinhole leaks from corrosion, higher material cost.
PEX.Flexible, fewer fittings, quick to install.Must be protected from sunlight and some chemicals.
CPVC.Common in some homes, resists corrosion.Can become brittle with age and heat.
Galvanized steel.Common in older homes.Rusts from the inside, restricts flow.
Polybutylene.Gray plastic found in some homes.Known to fail at fittings, plan to replace.

We recommend a material based on your house, your budget and what the existing lines look like. We do not push one material for every job.

Why Homes from the 1970s Deserve a Look

Age matters more than any single symptom.

Technology Park began in the late 1960s, and neighborhoods such as Peachtree Station, Chattahoochee Station and Spalding Corners followed in the 1970s. Houses from that era are now more than fifty years old. Supply lines, valves and fixtures from that period are at an age where failures become common.

That does not mean every old house needs new pipes. It does mean that a plumber should inspect the lines, test pressure and look at what material was used. If you see gray plastic or old galvanized steel, plan on repiping sooner rather than later.

Water here comes from the Gwinnett County Department of Water Resources and reaches your house through a meter in the yard. Everything past the meter is yours to maintain, so the age of your own plumbing is your decision.

What Whole-House Repiping Involves

The work has a clear sequence, and we explain it before we start.

  1. We inspect and quote. The plumber maps the lines, notes access and explains the options.
  2. We schedule around you. Most homes need the water off for part of each work day, and we plan around that.
  3. We open small access points. New lines run through attics, crawlspaces and short wall openings where possible.
  4. We connect and test. Each section is pressure tested before the wall is closed.
  5. We restore the water and walk you through it. You see every fixture working before we leave.

Repiping is where the 2024 International Plumbing Code (IPC) matters most, because it is the base of Georgia's state minimum plumbing code in effect since January 1, 2025. Ask the Peachtree Corners Building Division whether your project needs a permit.

Living in the House During Repiping

Plan for a few disrupted days, not weeks of chaos.

Water is off for part of each day, so keep bottled water and plan showers around work hours. Move furniture away from walls where access holes will go. Cover valuables that sit near the work zones, because drywall dust travels.

Patching drywall is a separate trade in most cases. We tell you which openings we make and leave them ready for patching. If a repair needs a permit, we explain that during the quote. Permit rules vary by job, so ask the Peachtree Corners Building Division at City Hall, 310 Technology Parkway, 678-691-1200.
