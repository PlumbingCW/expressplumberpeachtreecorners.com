Slab Leak Repair Cost: What Moves the Price

Slab leak repair cost depends on where the pipe is, how deep the break sits and which repair path fits the damage. No honest plumber can name a final price before the leak is located.

The five drivers below show what shapes a quote, and the comparison tips help you read two estimates side by side.

Why There Is No Single Number

Slab leaks come in many forms. A pinhole in a copper line near the surface is a very different job from a break under a bathroom with tile above it. The price follows the work, and the work follows the conditions.

Any plumber who quotes a firm number over the phone is guessing. The responsible first step is to locate the leak, confirm that it sits in a supply line and see what surrounds it. That step is usually a small fraction of the repair and it changes everything that follows.

This guide explains the cost drivers in plain terms. It does not list dollar figures, because figures from other houses will not match yours and a wrong anchor leads to bad decisions.

Signs You May Have a Slab Leak

Slab leaks hide, but the house usually tells you. Look for patterns that point under the floor.

  • A warm spot on a floor, especially near a hot water line.
  • The sound of running water when every fixture is off.
  • A water bill that rises with no change in habits.
  • Cracks or damp patches in flooring or baseboards.
  • Low pressure at fixtures that used to run strong.

Some homes have basements or crawl spaces, and a slab leak applies only to a home built on a concrete slab. If you are unsure how your house is built, a plumber can tell you in a few minutes. Our leak detection work starts by confirming where the water is going.

Run the County's Two-Hour Meter Test First

Gwinnett County Department of Water Resources describes a simple leak check. Turn off every faucet and appliance, then watch the meter's leak-indicator wheel for about two hours. The meter sits in the front yard under a metal cover.

If the wheel moves, water is escaping somewhere on the house side of the meter, and that stretch is the owner's responsibility. The county also offers a leak adjustment on a bill once every 12 months, so keep your meter photos and plumber's report. These records help whether or not the leak turns out to be under the slab.

Source: Gwinnett County Department of Water Resources leak detection and billing guidance.

Five factors account for most of the difference between one slab leak quote and another.

The restoration line is the one people forget. Repairing the pipe is one job, and putting the floor back is another. Ask whether your quote includes patching the slab and who handles flooring.

Three Repair Paths and What Each Involves

Plumbers choose among three approaches, and the right one depends on the pipe and the layout.

Spot repair. The crew cuts through the floor, exposes the pipe, repairs the break and patches the slab. This suits a single failure in an otherwise sound line. It costs the least in plumbing labor and the most in surprise if the pipe is failing elsewhere.

Reroute. The crew abandons the leaking line under the slab and runs a new line through walls or the attic. This avoids more concrete work and can be faster when the old line is deep.

Repipe. When leaks keep appearing, a repiping project replaces the supply lines throughout the house. It costs more up front and ends the cycle of repeated repairs.

A good plumber explains why a path fits your home and what it leaves for later.

Repairs should follow Georgia's state minimum plumbing code, based on the 2024 International Plumbing Code (IPC). Permit rules vary by job, so ask the Peachtree Corners Building Division what your project requires before you accept a scope.

Why Detection Comes Before Pricing

Leak location equipment saves money by shrinking the hole. Listening gear picks up the sound of escaping water through concrete. Thermal cameras show warm patches where a hot line is leaking. Tracer gas and pressure tests confirm which line is at fault.

Without detection, a crew may open the wrong spot, and each extra square foot of slab adds cost. With detection, the crew cuts a small access hole above the break.

You can also use detection results to compare quotes. A written report that names the pipe, the location and the likely path lets two plumbers quote the same job instead of two different guesses. Call a slab leak repair specialist who offers both detection and repair so the handoff is smooth.

Insurance and Paperwork

Homeowners policies treat slab leaks differently. Some cover the sudden damage from a leak and some exclude damage under the foundation. A few cover the access cost but not the pipe repair.

Read your policy before work starts, or call your agent and ask in plain words what is covered. Keep photos of the damage, the leak detection report and every invoice.

If you plan to ask the county for a billing adjustment, request it with the plumber's report in hand. Gwinnett County offers one adjustment every 12 months, so a second leak in the same year may not qualify. Homeowners in Duluth and Johns Creek deal with different utilities, so check your own water provider's rules.

How to Compare Two Quotes

Two estimates often look far apart. The gap usually comes from scope, not from one plumber being greedy.

  • Does each quote name the repair path: spot repair, reroute or repipe?
  • Does it include concrete patching and flooring, or only the plumbing?
  • Does it include a pressure test after the repair?
  • Does it state what happens if a second leak turns up?
  • Is the price written down before work begins?

Ask each plumber to walk you through the answers. The one who explains clearly and shows you the detection results is usually the one who knows the house.
